# The Art of Small Experiments

> [!abstract]
> When uncertainty is high, the best next step is often not a bigger plan—it is a smaller experiment.

## The idea

A small experiment is a low-cost action designed to replace an assumption with evidence. Instead of asking, “What is the perfect decision?” ask:

> What is the smallest thing I can do to learn whether this direction is promising?

This works because many difficult decisions are difficult only while they remain abstract. Reality provides feedback that planning alone cannot.

## A useful structure

Every experiment should define four things:

1. **Belief** — What do I currently think is true?
2. **Action** — What small step will test that belief?
3. **Signal** — What evidence would support or weaken it?
4. **Boundary** — How much time, money, or effort am I willing to spend?

For example:

- **Belief:** People would find a weekly project digest useful.
- **Action:** Send a hand-written digest to five people for two weeks.
- **Signal:** At least three read it and ask to receive another.
- **Boundary:** Four hours total, with no automation yet.

## Why small matters

Small experiments create several advantages:

- Failure stays inexpensive.
- Feedback arrives quickly.
- It is easier to change direction without defending sunk costs.
- Repeated evidence builds confidence more reliably than enthusiasm does.

The goal is not to avoid commitment forever. It is to make commitment *after* the most important uncertainty has been reduced.

> [!tip] A practical rule
> If an experiment takes so long that you could forget the original question, shrink it.

## Questions to ask

- What assumption could make this entire idea fail?
- Can I test that assumption without building the full solution?
- What result would honestly change my mind?
- What am I learning, rather than merely producing?
